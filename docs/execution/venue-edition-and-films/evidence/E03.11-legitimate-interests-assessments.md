## DRAFT STATUS. READ THIS BEFORE ANYTHING ELSE.

**What this is.** Three written legitimate interests assessments, drafted by an AI
agent under decision D-016, in the structure a supervisory authority expects:
purpose test, necessity test, balancing test, safeguards, outcome.

**What it is not.** Not legal advice. Not a reviewed instrument. Not a compliance
statement. An assessment that reaches "the interest is not overridden" is a
**recorded reasoning**, not a finding that the processing is lawful.

## 0. Why these exist at all, and why they are written before the basis is settled

Role map §12 item 1 records the lawful basis for private workspace content as
**unresolved** between Article 6(1)(f) with a written assessment, and Article
6(1)(b) plus 6(1)(f). It then gives the instruction this document follows:

> **Write the assessment regardless.** It is cheap, it forces the balancing to be
> thought through, it is required if the first reading is right, and it costs
> nothing if the second is. **Never rely on Article 6(1)(b) alone for anything
> touching third-party data.**

That last sentence is the load-bearing one. Article 6(1)(b) is a contract basis
and a guest has no contract with anyone. Whatever the answer for the couple's own
data, the guest's data cannot rest on 6(1)(b), so the balancing has to be done
anyway.

**Three assessments, because they are three different processings with three
different balances.**

| | Processing | Who is affected |
|---|---|---|
| **LIA-1** | Hosting a couple's private planning workspace, including personal data about guests, family and suppliers | The couple, and third parties who never contacted Signal Studio |
| **LIA-2** | Venue invitation, couple redemption, and suppressed venue-facing activation reporting | Couples |
| **LIA-3** | Founder outreach to wedding venues | Named individuals at venue businesses |

**One standing limit on all three.** An Article 6 legitimate interest does not lift
the Article 9 prohibition on special-category data. The conditions are additive.
Where LIA-1 reaches a positive outcome on Article 6, that outcome says nothing
about the Article 9 subset, which has no available condition (role map §4). The
two are kept apart deliberately, because merging them is how a document like this
becomes untrue.

## LIA-1 · Hosting a couple's private workspace, including third-party data

### 1.1 Purpose test

**What is the interest?** To provide a private wedding planning workspace that a
couple asked for and a venue paid for, and to keep it available, intact, secure
and recoverable for the term.

**Whose interest?** Signal Studio's commercial interest in operating the product,
the couple's interest in having it work, and the venue's interest in the gift it
paid for being real.

**Is it legitimate?** Yes, on any ordinary reading. Providing a requested service
is the plainest legitimate interest there is.

**How is the third-party data implicated?** A wedding workspace is mostly other
people's names. Guests, family members and suppliers appear in task titles,
descriptions and notes. None of them contacted Signal Studio and none of them
agreed to anything. **This is the part of the processing the assessment exists
for.** The interest in hosting the couple's own data is easy; the interest in
hosting a named guest's details is where the balance actually sits.

**What would be lost if the processing stopped?** The product would not exist. A
planning tool that cannot hold the names of the people being planned for is not a
planning tool.

### 1.2 Necessity test

**Is the processing necessary to achieve the interest?**

- Hosting the content the couple types: **yes**. There is no less intrusive way to
  store text a user typed than to store it.
- Holding third-party names inside that content: **yes, and unavoidably**. The
  couple decides who to name; Signal Studio cannot deliver the service and
  simultaneously refuse to hold the words.
- Backups and security logging: **yes**, and they are the ordinary Article 32
  incidents of hosting anything.

**Is there a less intrusive route?** Three were considered.

1. **Structured fields instead of free text.** Would reduce the Article 9 exposure
   specifically, and role map §4 sets out the guest-supplied channel that does it
   properly. It would not remove third-party names from free text, because a
   couple will still type "ring Aoife about the flowers".
2. **Client-side encryption so Signal Studio cannot read anything.** Genuinely
   less intrusive, and it would make it impossible to answer a guest's erasure
   request, which role map Scenario 1 establishes Signal Studio must be able to
   do. It trades one duty for another rather than reducing harm.
3. **Shorter retention.** Not an alternative to the processing, but a real
   reduction in its extent, and it is adopted as a safeguard below.

**Conclusion:** necessary, with the extent reducible by retention and by the
structured guest channel.

### 1.3 Balancing test

**The reasonable expectations of the people affected.**

- **The couple** expects a planning tool to hold what they type. Expectation is
  met.
- **A guest** has no expectation at all, because they do not know the product
  exists. Recital 47 makes reasonable expectation central, and a person who has
  never heard of the controller cannot expect anything. **This weighs against
  Signal Studio and the assessment does not pretend otherwise.**
- **A supplier** is a professional named in a business context, and is the one
  affected person in the map likely to find the service and complain. Role map
  Scenario 2 records this.

**The nature of the data.** Ordinary personal data for the most part: names,
contact details, roles, dates. Foreseeably some of it is Article 9: health,
religious belief revealed indirectly, disability revealed through requirements
about physical access. Foreseeably some of it concerns children, because guest
lists routinely do, and Article 6(1)(f) itself names children as weighting the
balance.

**The likely impact.**

- **Low in the ordinary case.** The content sits in a private workspace, is never
  disclosed to the venue, and is not used to profile anyone or to market to
  anyone.
- **Serious in the publication case.** One settings toggle publishes every task in
  a workspace, and titles and tags publish. A named guest and a health hint on a
  public page is the highest-severity shipped item in the system, in the role
  map's own words.
- **Mitigated by a decided position that is partly built.** D-033 decided R-031 as
  Option B: a wedding workspace is `noindex` by default and search visibility is
  an explicit couple opt-in. Role map §13 Finding 5 records that the opt-in has no
  storage yet, so the default is currently the whole behaviour.

**What tips the balance, and what would tip it back.**

The factors against Signal Studio are: no relationship with the affected person,
no notice to them, foreseeable special-category content, foreseeable children's
data, and a publication path that is one click wide.

The factors for: the data is supplied by someone with a real relationship to the
person, the purpose is the mundane one they would expect if asked, there is no
profiling, no secondary use, no advertising, no sale, no venue egress, and the
retention is bounded by the access term.

**Outcome, stated with its condition attached.**

> The interest is **not overridden for ordinary personal data in a private
> workspace**, provided the safeguards in §1.4 are in place. Three of those
> safeguards do not exist today, and until they do this outcome is a plan rather
> than a conclusion.

> The interest **does not resolve the Article 9 subset at all.** Article 9 is a
> prohibition lifted only by a listed condition, and role map §4 finds none
> available. Nothing in this assessment supplies one, and no document may cite
> this assessment as though it did.

### 1.4 Safeguards

| Safeguard | State |
|---|---|
| No venue egress. The sponsor allowlist is exhaustive and enforced server-side | **BUILT** |
| No profiling, no secondary use, no advertising, no sale of personal data | **BUILT**, in the sense that no such path exists |
| Tenant isolation, verified by a negative test per object | **BUILT.** See `evidence/E03.04-security-schedule.md` §1 |
| Descriptions excluded from the published projection | **BUILT.** `app/src/lib/public-task.ts` |
| Attachments excluded from every published projection, enforced by an allowlist that rebuilds the object field by field | **BUILT.** Role map §13 Finding 4 |
| Wedding workspaces `noindex` by default | **PARTLY BUILT.** The opt-in has no storage |
| An Article 14 notice for people named by someone else, at a stable URL | **NOT BUILT.** The live notices have no Article 14 content at all |
| A route by which a named third party can ask for removal, resolving without an account | **NOT BUILT.** Role map founder decision 1 |
| A hard no-publication rule at title and tag level for special-category content, in code, with a regression test | **NOT BUILT** |
| Shortest retention in the system for special-category content, guest contact details and children's data, purged at the end of the access term | **NOT BUILT.** Settled on the build regardless of basis (role map §12 item 3), and no mechanism exists |
| Per-item publish opt-in, or a plain statement that publishing a workspace publishes everything in it | **NOT BUILT.** Role map founder decision 4 |

**The three that matter most, named so they are not lost in a table:** the Article
14 notice, the removal route, and the retention purge. Role map founder decision 1
records the first two as the cheapest and highest-value items in the whole map.

### 1.5 Review

Reviewed when any of these changes: the publication default, the retention rule,
the R-017 template decision, or the arrival of a guest-supplied structured
channel. Reviewed in any event before the first sponsored couple redeems.

## LIA-2 · Invitation, redemption and venue-facing activation reporting

### 2.1 Purpose test

**What is the interest?** Signal Studio's and the venue's shared commercial
interest in a couple receiving, opening and using a workspace the venue gifted;
and the venue's interest in knowing whether what it paid for is being taken up.

**Is it legitimate?** Yes for both limbs. Delivering a gift someone paid for, and
telling the payer whether it landed, are ordinary commercial interests.

**Note on the open question.** Role map §12 item 2 records the basis for
invitation and redemption as unresolved between Article 6(1)(f) for both parties
and Article 6(1)(b) inside the venue's booking relationship. This assessment is
written for the 6(1)(f) reading because that reading is the one that requires it.
Writing it costs nothing if 6(1)(b) wins.

### 2.2 Necessity test

- **Invitation:** the venue must supply an identifier for a couple in order to
  invite them. There is no less intrusive way to send someone a specific thing.
- **Redemption:** the couple's own act.
- **Reporting:** the venue's interest is in take-up, not in individuals. That can
  be met with aggregates, which is materially less intrusive than any per-couple
  view. Therefore the aggregate form is **necessary and the per-couple form is
  not**, and the per-couple form is refused.

### 2.3 Balancing test

**Reasonable expectations.** A couple who receives a workspace from their venue
expects the venue to know they were invited, and would not be surprised that the
venue knows whether the gift was opened. That expectation is the strongest factor
in this assessment.

**What the couple would not expect**, and therefore what is refused: that the
venue can see their planning content, their task counts, when they were last
active, or any signal that identifies them individually inside a report.

**The specific harm this processing can cause.** Aggregate is not anonymous here.
The venue holds the re-identification key because it knows which couples it
invited. "Three of your six couples activated" tells the venue something specific
about the three who did not. Role map §7 sets this out and it is the reason the
suppression floor exists.

**Two facts that make the balance better than it was when the role map was
written.** Role map §13 Finding 1 records that the suppression controls were
defective at that time: the behavioural floor guarded the population and not the
count, so a value of one could be published against a population of forty; and the
rate floor had no production caller at all. Both are now fixed and
mutation-tested. A live report with defective suppression is a disclosure rather
than a projection, so the balance genuinely moved.

**Outcome.**

> The interest is **not overridden**, for the invitation, for redemption, and for
> **aggregate reporting subject to the suppression controls**. It **is** overridden
> for any per-couple signal, and no such signal is provided.

### 2.4 Safeguards

| Safeguard | State |
|---|---|
| Two-sided behavioural suppression floor: withheld when the value is under three **or** when the population minus the value is under three | **BUILT and mutation-tested** |
| Rate suppression below five eligible activations, with the denominator travelling with the value | **BUILT** |
| A withheld cell reads "not enough activity to report yet", never "fewer than 3" | **BUILT** |
| No per-couple row, no ranking, no last-active, no task count, no content signal | **BUILT** |
| No filter combination can narrow a view to one couple | **BUILT** |
| Article 26 arrangement with the venue, including single-point-of-contact handling | **DRAFTED**, at `evidence/E03.04-data-processing-agreement.md` §4.3. Not signed by anyone |
| **Telling couples at signup that activation data about them goes to the venue** | **NOT BUILT.** Role map §7 records it as owed and missing. Without it the couple's expectation is being assumed rather than informed, which weakens the strongest factor in §2.3 |
| An Article 21 objection route for a couple who does not want to be counted | **NOT BUILT** |
| Wedding date changes never surfaced anywhere | **BUILT as policy** (D-011 point 1) |

**The gap that most affects this assessment** is the missing Article 13
disclosure. The balance in §2.3 leans on what a couple would reasonably expect,
and the cheapest way to make an expectation reasonable is to tell them.

### 2.5 Review

Reviewed when the venue's role label is settled (role map §12 item 5), when the
Article 13 disclosure ships, and before any per-venue adoption evidence is turned
on. `app/src/app/api/internal/partner-stats/route.ts` applies no floor at all and
is founder-only today; it becomes a disclosure the moment per-venue evidence is
enabled, and this assessment does not cover it.

## LIA-3 · Founder outreach to wedding venues

### 3.1 Purpose test

**What is the interest?** Signal Studio's interest in telling 25 Greater Limerick
wedding venues that a product built for them exists, and in finding the ones that
want it.

**Is it legitimate?** Yes. Business-to-business outreach to a named professional
about a service relevant to their business is the paradigm example given for
direct marketing as a legitimate interest in Recital 47.

**Who is affected?** Individuals at venue businesses: an owner, a manager, a
wedding coordinator. Their name and work email are personal data even though the
relationship is commercial.

### 3.2 Necessity test

**Is outreach necessary?** To reach 25 specific venues in one region, yes.
Advertising does not reach a named coordinator at a named house.

**Is the data used necessary?** Contact details come from published business
sources: the venue's own website, its published wedding enquiry address. No
enrichment, no purchased list, no scraped personal address, no inference about the
individual. The minimum is a name, a role and a work address.

**Is a less intrusive route available?** A general advertisement would not reach
the cohort. A physical letter is more intrusive and was ruled out for other
reasons (D-018). Outreach at this scale and specificity is the least intrusive
route to the interest.

### 3.3 Balancing test

**Reasonable expectations.** A wedding coordinator whose enquiry address is
published on their venue's own website expects to be contacted about weddings by
suppliers. That is the address's purpose.

**Nature of the data.** Business contact data about an identified professional in
their professional capacity. No special categories. No children. No profiling.

**Impact.** Low. The message is a personal email from a named person with a real
reply address, sent by hand, to at most 25 businesses, capped at four touches.

**What would make it worse, and is deliberately absent:** open pixels, link
tracking, a purchased list, a personal rather than a work address, automation that
cannot tell it has been asked to stop, and any manufactured urgency.

**Outcome.**

> The interest is **not overridden**, provided the safeguards in §3.4 are in place.
> The one that carries the outcome is the suppression list, and it currently exists
> as a policy rather than as a file.

### 3.4 Safeguards

| Safeguard | State |
|---|---|
| Contact details from published business sources only | **POLICY**, recorded in `evidence/E11.05-07-outreach-email.md` |
| No open pixels, no link tracking | **POLICY**, recorded in `studio/docs/email-system/email-inventory.md` |
| A plain opt-out in the founder's own words rather than a footer | **BUILT as copy.** The line is recorded against D-013 point 4 |
| Stopping rule at four touches; an explicit no stops at one | **POLICY** |
| Suppression list honoured before every send, permanent, never pruned | **POLICY ONLY. There is no store.** See `evidence/E03.11-retention-deletion-and-discontinuation.md` §6.1 |
| Identity of the sender is a real person with a monitored reply address | **BUILT.** `hello@signalstudio.ie`, and outbound mail authenticates: SPF, DKIM for Resend and Clerk, and DMARC published at `p=none` |
| An Article 13 notice available to a recipient who asks what is held about them | **NOT BUILT** as a specific outreach notice |

### 3.5 Review

Reviewed before each cohort is contacted, and immediately if the outreach volume,
the source of the contact data, or the automation of sending changes. The
assessment is written for hand-sent founder mail to 25 named businesses. It does
not carry to a larger list or to an automated sequence.

## 4. Points a solicitor must settle

1. Is Article 6(1)(f) the correct basis for hosting a couple's private workspace,
   or is it Article 6(1)(b) for the couple's own data with 6(1)(f) only for the
   third-party content within it (role map §12 item 1)?
2. Where a controller's Article 6 balancing passes but no Article 9(2) condition
   exists for a subset of the same data, is a documented harm-reduction position
   with a stated end date a defensible place to stand, and for how long?
3. Is a legitimate interests assessment whose safeguards are largely not yet built
   worth more or less than no assessment at all, from the point of view of a
   supervisory authority reading it after an incident?
4. Does the couple's own status under the household exemption change the answer in
   LIA-1, given that Article 2(2)(c) requires no connection to a commercial
   activity and the workspace sits inside a paid venue booking (role map §12 item
   4, untested)?
5. Is the balance in LIA-2 sustainable while couples are not told at signup that
   activation data about them goes to the venue, or does the missing disclosure
   defeat the reasonable-expectations argument the assessment relies on?
6. Does S.I. 336 of 2011 as amended treat a named individual's work email at a
   business as a subscriber for electronic marketing purposes, and does that
   change the LIA-3 outcome?
7. Should each of these assessments be published, kept internal, or summarised in
   the privacy notice?
