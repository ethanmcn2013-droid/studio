## 1. The one rule that cannot be broken, and why it comes first

ADR-008 §0 states the central architectural claim, and everything below is downstream of it.

> There are two axes, not one, and they are orthogonal. **Access** governs what the couple
> can do with their workspace. **Sponsorship** governs the commercial link between a venue
> and that workspace. **No sponsorship transition may ever change the access state.**

That is D-020 point 2, which Signal Studio states unprompted in the outreach email and in
the agreement: *any workspace already redeemed keeps its full term plus Keepsake, whatever
happens to the licence. Only new issuance stops.*

**It is enforced rather than asserted.** `accessAfterSponsorshipEvent` in
`studio/src/lib/venue-lifecycle.ts` returns the access state unchanged for every sponsorship
event, and `venue-lifecycle.test.ts` asserts that across every state-and-event pair. A
future change that breaks the promise fails a test rather than reaching a venue.

Every rule drafted below is checked against that invariant. Where a drafted rule would have
moved access on a sponsorship event, the rule was rewritten, not the invariant.

### Case 1 — the couple postpones the wedding

**What happens today.** D-022 point 3 is implemented and tested. `extendCoupleAccessForWeddingDate`
in `app/src/server/db/couple-access-term.ts` recomputes
`max(redemption + 548 days, wedding + 90 days)` and **extends, never shortens**. A
correction to an earlier date leaves the term where it was. Both repositories carry the
arithmetic and a differential test holds them identical.

**And the verified gap, which is larger than the rule.** The header comment of that same
file states it, and it was re-read on 2026-08-04:

> No wedding date reaches this code in production today, for three reasons, and every
> sponsored couple therefore falls back to the 548-day floor.

The three reasons: the capture is behind the `contextualOnboarding` flag whose production
default is off; Venue Edition redemption **skips onboarding entirely** and deep-links
to `/app/tasks?welcome=venue&v=<slug>`; and `workspaces.primary_date` is written at
workspace creation only, so no action updates it afterwards and the postponement path
cannot be triggered from the product at all.

**Drafted rule.**

> A couple's access always runs to the later of eighteen months from the day they opened
> their workspace and three months past their wedding day. If the wedding moves later,
> access moves with it, automatically, without anyone asking. If a date is corrected to an
> earlier day, access does not move.
>
> Where the product does not hold a wedding date, the term runs from the day the workspace
> was opened, and Signal Studio extends it on request or on learning the date. Nobody loses
> access before their own wedding.

**State: rule only, and the last sentence is an operations commitment.** The first paragraph
is implemented; the second exists because the first cannot fire today. See `[DP-1]`.

### Case 2 — the couple cancels the wedding

**What happens today.** Nothing. There is no cancellation concept anywhere in either
repository, no field, no route and no rule. ADR-008 §11 does not list it, because the
sources it harvested did not either.

**Drafted rule, at the safer reading for the couple.**

> A cancelled wedding changes nothing about the couple's workspace. The term runs as it
> was, and it ends in Keepsake like any other.
>
> Signal Studio does not ask why a wedding did not happen, does not record a reason, and
> does not tell the venue anything it would not otherwise have been told.
>
> A venue may release the sponsorship. If it does, the couple keeps every word of their
> content, and the venue's name comes off the workspace within twenty-four hours.

**Why the safer reading is this one.** The alternative shapes all involve Signal Studio
learning something about the worst week of somebody's year and then doing something with
it. Doing nothing is both the kinder rule and the one that needs no new field, no new
disclosure and no new lawful basis. D-011 point 1 already refuses to make a postponement a
dashboard event; a cancellation is the same principle with more at stake.

**State: rule only.** The release half is a rule the machine expresses
(`sponsorBrandingVisible`, `brandingRemovalDeadlineMs`) and no code calls. See case 5.

### Case 3 — the couple changes venue mid-planning

**What happens today.** Nothing is representable. `workspaceSponsorships` is **insert-only**:
verified on 2026-08-04, one `insert` at `app/src/server/actions/planning.ts:939` and no
`update` anywhere in `app/src`. There is no release action, no re-point, and no way for a
workspace to carry a second sponsorship or to change the one it has.

**Drafted rule, at the safer reading for the couple.**

> The couple keeps the workspace they have been working in. They are never asked to start
> again.
>
> The first venue may release the sponsorship. Its name comes off within twenty-four hours.
> Access is untouched: the term runs exactly as it did.
>
> The new venue may send a fresh invitation. The couple does not have to redeem it, and
> nothing is lost if they do not.
>
> The first venue is told that the sponsorship ended. It is not told where the couple went,
> and Signal Studio does not put a reason on the record.

**The one thing this rule cannot yet do.** If the couple redeems the second venue's
invitation, there is no mechanism to attach that sponsorship to the existing workspace, and
redemption creates a new one. **Two workspaces for one wedding is a worse outcome for the
couple than either alternative.** The two ways out, and neither is chosen here:

- **(a)** Allow a workspace's sponsorship to be re-pointed by an operator, keeping one
  workspace and one term. Smaller product change, needs an audited operator action.
- **(b)** Allow a second, superseding sponsorship row, keeping the history. Truer record,
  larger change to every projection that assumes one sponsor per workspace.

**State: undecided on the mechanism, drafted on the promise.** `[DP-2]`.

### Case 4 — the venue does not renew, or its licence lapses

**What happens today.** This is the best-covered case in the register and it is covered
properly. `canIssueNewSponsorship` refuses new issuance for a lapsed licence and stops
there. `accessAfterSponsorshipEvent` returns the access state unchanged for
`licence_lapses`, asserted across every state-and-event pair. `LIFECYCLE_FIXTURES` carries a
lapsed-licence fixture with access unmoved, on a pinned clock, and a released-sponsorship
fixture with access unmoved. Those two fixtures are the survival promise, loadable on a
screen.

**Drafted rule, and it is the ratified sentence rather than a new one.**

> A workspace a couple has already opened keeps its full term, and its Keepsake afterwards,
> whatever happens to the venue's licence. Only new invitations stop.
>
> The couple is not told that their venue stopped paying, and nothing in their workspace
> changes on the day it happens. The venue's name comes off only if the venue releases the
> workspace, which is a separate act.

**State: covered.** D-020 point 2, `venue-lifecycle.ts`, `venue-lifecycle.test.ts`.

### Case 5 — venue-workspace unlinking, the release

**What happens today.** The mechanism is defined and proved, and nothing calls it.
`workspaceSponsorships.status` carries `active | revoked | expired` with a CHECK constraint
and a `revokedAt` column. `sponsorBrandingVisible` and
`brandingRemovalDeadlineMs` (`BRANDING_REMOVAL_DEADLINE_MS = 24 * 60 * 60 * 1000`) express
the rule. **There is no release action, no twenty-four-hour branding-removal job and no
venue-facing control**, because the table is insert-only and there is no venue-authenticated
route to put a control on (R-043).

**Drafted rule.**

> A venue may release a workspace it sponsored. Release does three things and only three:
> it ends the commercial link, it takes the venue's name off the workspace within
> twenty-four hours, and it stops that workspace counting toward anything the venue is
> shown.
>
> It does not touch the couple's access, their content, their term or their Keepsake. It is
> not a way to take a workspace back.

**What the venue's name actually is, so nobody drafts more than exists.** D-027 point 3:
the venue's **name only**. No logo, no venue-written welcome message, no co-branding.
Verified: the only surfaces the venue identity reaches are the dismissible welcome card
rendering `Compliments of {sponsorName}` and the Settings plan page.

**State: rule only. No writer.** `[DP-3]`.

### Case 6 — one partner deletes their account, and the shared workspace goes with it (R-023)

**This is a verified, live, currently shippable failure and it belongs to this task by
founder decision.** D-027 point 1: *"It needs a product answer to 'what does delete my
account mean when the work is shared', which is the same question E03.10 asks about
separation, so the two are answered together rather than separately."*

**What happens today, verified on 2026-08-04.**
`app/src/server/account-erasure.ts` selects every workspace where
`workspaces.ownerUserId` equals the erasing user and hard-deletes the whole thing: tasks,
comments, attachments, resources, notifications, share links, share-link visits and
entitlements, regardless of who created them. `app/src/app/api/account/delete/route.ts` runs
it immediately and irreversibly, then deletes the Clerk user.

`app/src/server/actions/settings.ts` enforces a one-active-owner floor, so a co-owner cannot
be **demoted** out of existence. Nothing stops the account holding `ownerUserId` from
calling account deletion and taking the other person's work with it. **The unit of this
product is a couple. Two people, one wedding, one workspace.**

**Three options. This document recommends one and does not choose it.**

| | What it does | What it costs | What it risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Refuse** | Deletion is refused while another owner exists. The person is told to transfer ownership or remove the other owner first. | Least code. | **Refusing an erasure request is the wrong answer to Article 17.** A person cannot be told to negotiate with an ex before they can leave a service. Recorded and not recommended. |
| **B. Delete the person, keep the workspace** *(recommended)* | The deleting person's account, identity, membership and personal data go. `ownerUserId` transfers to the remaining owner. The workspace and its content stay. The deleting person's authored content stays, because it is also the other person's wedding record. | A real change to `account-erasure.ts`: scope the workspace delete to workspaces with no other active owner, and add an ownership transfer. | The deleting person's contributions remain in a workspace they no longer control. That is a genuine tension with Article 17 and it is an open legal point. |
| **C. Delete and warn** | Current behaviour, with a confirmation naming the other person and what will be destroyed. | Smallest change. | It converts a defect into an informed defect. One person can still destroy another person's wedding record, now with a dialog. |

**Why B is recommended.** Both people are data subjects and both have rights over the
workspace. Option A denies the requester's right. Option C denies the other person's. Option
B satisfies the requester's erasure of **their own personal data**, which is what Article 17
attaches to, without destroying a second person's record of their own wedding. It is also
the only option that survives the sentence a founding venue would have to read in a
newspaper.

**State: undecided.** `[DP-4]`. D-027 point 1 dates it: **fixed before any venue is
contacted**, not release-blocking.

### Case 7 — the couple separates and both want the workspace

**The role map records this as CANNOT ANSWER, and this document does not create a rule.**
Role map Scenario 4 and §12 item 9. The founder decides.

**The shape of the problem, stated once.** Each party is a data subject of their own
contributions. Both hold Article 15 and Article 20 rights over what they put in. One party's
Article 17 request over shared content collides directly with the other's rights. Signal
Studio should not adjudicate ownership of a relationship, and a rule improvised during a
dispute is a rule written by whoever shouted first.

**And the correction that makes it harder than it looks.** The one proposal on the record
was: freeze every public surface, give both parties an export, refuse destructive action on
either party's unilateral instruction. Correction 2 of the role map breaks its first limb.
**Freezing an indexed page leaves it indexed.** A wedding workspace on `/p` is `noindex` by
default today (D-033), which reduces this, but `robots.txt` and `noindex` are requests, not
locks, and a URL already shared into a preview service is already out. So a freeze needs a
third state, **unpublish and de-index on notice of a dispute**, and that is itself a
destructive action taken on one party's instruction, which contradicts the rest of the
proposal.

**Four options.**

| | The rule | For it | Against it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Freeze and dual export** | On notice of a dispute: no destructive action on either party's sole instruction; both parties get a full export; the workspace stays readable to both. | Neutral. Nobody loses their record. Uses the export that already exists. | Leaves a published page live during the dispute, which is often the thing one party most wants down. Needs a "notice of a dispute" concept that does not exist. |
| **B. Unpublish first, then freeze** *(recommended)* | On notice of a dispute: unpublish immediately and de-index, then freeze and dual-export as in A. | Takes down the surface strangers can see, which is the only part with a third party on it. Reversible: republishing is one toggle. Consistent with acting unilaterally on published surfaces for a guest's request. | It is a destructive-looking action on one party's word. Mitigated by being fully reversible and by affecting no content. |
| **C. Owner of record wins** | Whoever holds `ownerUserId` keeps the workspace. The other party gets an export and loses access. | Deterministic. Needs almost no new code. | `ownerUserId` is set at creation and never reassigned. It records who clicked first, not who owns the wedding. It would hand a wedding to whichever partner happened to open the invitation email. |
| **D. No rule** | Status quo. Handle each case as it comes. | Nothing to build. | It is not "no rule". Today's behaviour is case 6: whoever holds `ownerUserId` can delete everything. That is option C with a delete button. |

**Recommendation: B.** It is A plus the one action that protects the person who is not in
the argument, it is reversible, and it does not require Signal Studio to decide who is
right about anything.

**What this document will not do.** It will not put a separation rule into the couple terms
before the founder picks one. E03.05 §3 clause 5 therefore says only that a rule exists and
where to find it, with the link left as a blank. `[DP-5]`.

### Case 8 — a guest or a supplier asks to be removed

**Covered by drafting elsewhere, recorded here so the register is complete.** ADR-008 §11
cases 18 and 19 are both marked uncovered, and the route is drafted in **E03.05 §4**, under
"People in your plan who never signed up", following the corrected shape of role map
Scenario 1: restrict on receipt, act unilaterally and immediately on any published surface,
ask the couple with a deadline, execute if the deadline passes, hold a suppression record,
confirm to the requester.

**The unresolved design constraint this creates, and it is not this task's to settle.**
Signal Studio's own position is that it does not read a couple's workspace. A controller
that cannot reach the data it controls cannot answer a data subject. Role map §8 offers two
exits and both are builds: reword the promise to *"we do not, except on a logged, narrow,
request-triggered basis"* and build an audited access path, or move third-party and
special-category data into a separately governed store. **The second is the same build as
the guest-supplied channel in role map §4.** `[DP-6]`.

**State: rule drafted, nothing built.** No intake route, no identity rule, no response
commitment, and no contact address on any surface a guest would reach.

### Case 9 — the couple loses access to the account

**What happens today.** Nothing exists. ADR-008 §4 records a search of the whole workspace
finding no password-reset flow, no lost-access flow and no self-service ownership transfer.
Authentication is Clerk's hosted product and nothing in either repository builds recovery on
top of it. The nearest thing is `clerkIdDead` in the entitlements schema, an operator
data-repair flag for a Clerk account-merge edge case.

**Drafted rule.**

> If a couple loses access to the email address behind their account, they email
> hello@signalstudio.ie and Signal Studio restores access by hand, after checking against
> what the venue's invitation recorded.

**State: rule only, and it is a manual process with no documented identity check.**
`[BLANK-1]`: the identity check itself. This is the case most likely to be met in the wild
in the first year, and it has no owner. Recorded rather than resolved.

## 5. What is not built, stated once, plainly

1. **No release action.** `workspaceSponsorships` is insert-only. One insert, no update.
2. **No twenty-four-hour branding-removal job.**
3. **No venue-facing control of any kind**, because there is no venue-authenticated route
   anywhere (R-043).
4. **No wedding-date capture on the sponsored path**, so the grace half of the access term
   never fires.
5. **No date-change flow**, so postponement cannot be triggered from the product.
6. **No cancellation, venue-change or separation concept** in either schema.
7. **No ownership transfer**, and `ownerUserId` is never reassigned by any code path.
8. **No account recovery.**
9. **No guest or supplier intake route**, and no contact address on any surface a guest
   would reach.
10. **No Keepsake surface, download route or read-only enforcement.**
    `isPermittedInKeepsake` has zero production callers.

Every rule in section 2 is written to be true regardless of which of these gets built. None
of them describes a control that exists.

## 6. Open legal points

1. When one owner of a shared workspace requests erasure, does Article 17 reach content
   they authored inside a record that is also another data subject's, and can a controller
   retain it on Article 17(3) or on the other party's Article 15 and 20 rights?
2. Is refusing an erasure request until a co-owner is removed (option A in case 6) a lawful
   response under Article 12(5), or is it a refusal that requires the Article 12(4) route to
   the DPC?
3. On notice of a dispute between two owners, may Signal Studio unpublish a page on one
   party's instruction without the other's, and is that a restriction of processing under
   Article 18 or a unilateral act against the second party?
4. Where a couple's access term is stated in a document but the product cannot compute it,
   what is the obligation, and does an undertaking to extend on request discharge it?
5. Is a venue's release of a sponsorship a matter the couple must be told about under
   Article 13 or 14, given that the venue's name is removed from the couple's own screen as
   a result?
6. What identity check may Signal Studio require from a couple who has lost access to the
   account email, given that the only corroborating record is held by the venue that
   invited them, and does asking the venue disclose anything the venue is not entitled to?
7. Does telling a first venue that a sponsorship ended, when the reason is that the couple
   moved to a competitor, disclose anything about the couple that requires a basis of its
   own?
