## What it actually costs to serve a couple

Verified from your own cost datapack (`content/hq/finance/cost-model-datapack-2026-07-20.json`):

- Your fixed run rate at launch is **€250.49/month** at zero users.
- Your own model puts variable cost at **€0.00 at 0 users, €0.26/month at 100, €2.62/month at 1,000**.
- At Founding-25 scale the marginal cost of one more couple is genuinely **€0.00** — every couple fits inside allowances you have already paid for.

So infrastructure does not decide this. At 20 and 40 couples, all three models
land within €40 of each other and every one clears 90% margin. **Founder
attention decides it, and so does VAT.**

### Three corrections the first pass got wrong

1. **The unit was ambiguous.** €1.80 per couple was used as both a lifetime and
   an annual figure. Corrected: about **€0.10 per live workspace per month**
   base, **€0.39 heavy**. Over an 18-month term that is €1.80 base, €7 heavy.
   At steady state, concurrent workspaces are roughly 1.5× annual intake because
   the term outruns the year.
2. **The daily briefing is a base-case cost, not a heavy one.** It is promised
   to every couple in the sales narrative and it is a free-tier feature in code.
   Folding it in moves the realistic base case from 90–95% margin toward
   **60–75%**.
3. **VAT was absent from every figure.** See below. It is the largest single
   number on this page.

## The VAT problem, which is bigger than the entitlement question

Two findings, one of which reverses something I told you yesterday.

**You may already be required to register, regardless of turnover.** Irish
registration thresholds apply to supplies you *make*. A business that *receives*
services from suppliers established outside Ireland — Vercel, Clerk, Turso,
Resend, Stripe, Sentry, Anthropic, all of which you already buy — can become an
accountable person and must self-account under the reverse charge, **with no
turnover threshold at all**. If that is the position, VAT applies to the first
€1,000, not the €42,500th.

**The irreversible part is one sentence in the agreement.** If the founding
schedule says "€1,000" and is silent on VAT, and you later register, arguing the
price was always exclusive is weak against 25 signed documents that say nothing.
€1,000 becomes about **€813 net** — a permanent 19% cut across every founding
contract, and D-009's price lock means you cannot correct it.

> **State the price VAT-exclusive, with VAT added at the prevailing rate, in
> every agreement from the first draft.** That single sentence is the whole fix
> and it costs nothing. It is the most valuable line in this document.

**And I was wrong yesterday.** I told you zero budget meant no external
authority was available. That is not true. **Revenue's MyEnquiries is free** and
returns a written answer from the tax authority on a stated set of facts. So is
the Data Protection Commission's guidance route. For the VAT question
specifically, you can get an actual authoritative answer for nothing, and you
should.

## The three options

Costs below are per venue per year, at €1,000 revenue (€985 net of Stripe),
using the corrected per-workspace-month unit.

### A. Every booked couple — unlimited, fair-use shaped

**Rule.** A venue with a current paid licence may create a workspace for any
couple with a signed booking. No number in the commercial terms. The existing
`code_allotment` field becomes an internal issuance ceiling, raisable on request
without any change to price.

**To a venue:** *Every couple who books their wedding with you gets a workspace,
for as long as your licence is current. No seat count, no per-couple maths,
nothing for your coordinator to track.*

| Couples/yr | Base cost | Heavy cost | Contribution (base) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | €36 | €140 | €949 (95%) |
| 40 | €72 | €280 | €913 (93%) |
| 80 | €144 | €560 | €841 base / **€425 heavy (43%)** |

**Break-even:** 547 couples base, **~94 heavy** under the corrected unit — not
141. The largest venues in the region run 150–250 weddings. So unlimited is safe
by a wide margin for a median venue and **genuinely exposed at the top end**,
which is not what the first pass concluded.

**Pros.** Nothing new to count, maintain, invoice or dispute. Removes the
perverse incentive against the best venues. Survives the move to €1,500 without
renegotiation.

**Cons.** Gives up size-based pricing. Open-ended cost line, permanently
price-locked for the founding 25. "Fair use" is legally soft and is being
drafted without a solicitor.

### B. Sixty a year — bounded by a countable unit

**Rule.** Up to 60 workspaces per licence year, counted at issuance, no
rollover. Extra blocks of 20 at €100.

**Verdict: don't.** Three reviewers independently killed it.

- Counting at issuance makes non-redemption *the venue's loss* — hand out 60,
  35 redeem, your year is "used". That is metering, and the rational venue
  response is to ration codes, which is the exact behaviour you are trying to
  prevent.
- The top-up block **loses money in the heavy case**: 20 workspaces at €100
  against €140 of cost. Its one virtue — a closed downside — does not survive
  its own expansion mechanism.
- "A revoked code returns its headroom" **contradicts shipped code.** The mint
  counter is monotone by design, the reconciler asserts it, and your own metric
  dictionary has a required test named *"Revoked codes do not silently restore
  headroom."* Honouring the clause would make the venue's portal display
  "Needs reconciliation" instead of a number.
- Its failure mode is the worst renewal conversation available: a coordinator
  hits the ceiling in July, doesn't email, quietly stops handing codes out, and
  the portal shows 60/60 issued — which reads as success.

### C. One per wedding in your diary

**Rule.** One workspace per signed booking; the venue declares its annual
wedding count in the agreement, covered to that plus 20%.

**The good part is the arithmetic, not the rule.** €1,000 across 20 weddings is
**€50 a wedding**; across 40 it is €25; across 80 it is €12.50. Against a
€25,000–€35,000 wedding, that is the strongest line available and it belongs in
the cold email regardless of which model you pick.

**The bad part is asking for the number.** A venue owner asked to declare annual
wedding volume to a stranger on a first agreement will sometimes refuse, and
refusal kills the deal. Some will under-declare defensively, which makes your
first real dataset systematically wrong. And the operations reviewer found that
enforcing it would require couple-level booking attribution — which is exactly
what your Venue Portal privacy contract is built *not* to hold.

## Recommendation: A, corrected

Take **A**, sell it with **C's per-wedding arithmetic**, and add the five fixes
the reviewers found. Infrastructure is not the constraint; your attention is,
and A is the only option that adds nothing new to maintain. But it does not ship
as originally drafted.

**The five corrections, all required:**

1. **Price stated VAT-exclusive** in every document, from the first draft.
2. **Eligibility screen at the top end.** A venue running more than ~120
   weddings a year goes to the standard €1,500, not the founding rate. This is
   honest, it protects the price lock from its one real failure case, and it is
   easier to say before signature than after.
3. **Fair use notifies, never blocks.** The first draft paused issuance above
   150 codes — in a document that also says "you do not run out". Make it alert
   HQ and keep issuing. A numeric pause must never appear beside the word
   unlimited.
4. **A survival sentence, said unprompted.** *Any workspace already redeemed
   keeps its full term plus Keepsake, whatever happens to your licence. Only new
   issuance stops.* Every reviewer flagged this as the first question a venue
   asks on an annual prepay, and the answer converts the biggest objection into
   a trust proof.
5. **A release rule.** If a couple cancels or moves venue, the venue can release
   the workspace, the couple keeps their content in Keepsake, and your branding
   comes off within 24 hours. Otherwise a couple planning a wedding at a
   competitor keeps a workspace with your name on it.

Collect the venue's wedding count **after** signature as an onboarding field,
never as a contract term, with one line: *this sets your issuance ceiling, it
never sets your price, and it never changes at renewal.*

## What has to change in code before A is real

"Unlimited" is currently **unrepresentable**, verified in the repo:

- `src/lib/entitlements-db/codes.ts:56-60` — mint refuses a null allotment by
  design: *"a null allotment = not [permitted]"*.
- `src/app/hq/entitlements/OnboardVenueForm.tsx:63-67` — the onboarding form
  hard-requires a number, `min=1`, **`defaultValue={10}`**.

**So today's de facto entitlement is a form default of 10 that nobody decided,
against a live page promising "No seats. No per-couple maths."** Renaming the
field does not fix it. Four changes are needed: an unlimited representation the
mint accepts, suppression of the remaining-count and headroom warnings in the
portal for unlimited sponsors, the HQ near-allotment list filtered, and the form
default replaced with a computed ceiling.

## Two findings that are bigger than this decision

**1. D-010's grace rule cannot be minted today.** You ratified "18 months from
redemption, or 3 months past the wedding date, whichever is later". Verified in
code: `src/lib/venue-edition.ts:6` sets `VENUE_EDITION_COUPLE_ACCESS_DAYS = 548`
and `codes.ts:81-88` **throws on any Venue Edition mint whose duration is not
exactly 548**. Irish venues book 12–24 months out. A couple who books in March
2027 for a September 2028 wedding and redeems on the day they sign **loses
Signal Studio before their wedding** — in public, at your venue. The decision is
right; the code refuses it. Recorded as R-015.

**2. You are collecting Article 9 special-category data about people who never
agreed to anything.** Dietary and allergy notes reveal health and routinely
reveal religious belief. Verified: `app/src/lib/templates.generated.ts:108` ships
a **"Collect final dietary notes"** task in the wedding template, and
`app/src/app/the-wedding/page.tsx:26` advertises *"One reply to Glenmara House,
including dietary notes."* That is guest health data, entered by the couple, and
the page says it flows to the venue. There is no realistic lawful condition —
explicit consent is the only candidate and the guest is never asked. Recorded as
R-017. The cheapest fix is architectural, not legal: move dietary capture to a
guest-facing RSVP step so the guest supplies it themselves. That turns an
unanswerable problem into a solved one, and it is a better product.
