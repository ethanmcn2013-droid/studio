### Correction 1 — there is no in-product dietary flow, and no dietary field (R-017 restated)

Version 0.1 treated `app/src/app/the-wedding/page.tsx:26` as the product advertising
that dietary notes flow to the venue, and both blind lanes named it "the single fact
that most changes the role map."

**Verified wrong.** That line is demo copy on a public marketing page. It is an
example task in a fictional couple's plan reading *"Send the final menu choices —
one reply to Glenmara House, including dietary notes."* It describes the **couple
emailing the venue themselves**, outside the product.

**Verified further.** There is no structured dietary, allergy or accessibility field
anywhere in the system. A grep across `app/src` for `dietar|allerg|accessibilityNeed`
returns only demo fixtures (`src/app/lab/notes-delight/lab-fixtures.ts`,
`src/server/demo/tasks-demo.ts`, `src/modules/notes/server/demo/notes-demo.ts`,
`src/modules/timeline/lib/roadmap/demo-data.ts`), the marketing page above, and one
task title string.

**What is true and unchanged:** `app/src/lib/templates.generated.ts:108` ships a task
titled **"Collect final dietary notes"** with tags `["catering", "guests"]` in the
wedding template.

**The real shape.** The private workspace is an **unstructured free-text store** that
will foreseeably contain Article 9 data about third parties, and Signal Studio ships a
template instructing the couple to collect it. Shipping that template determines an
essential means (the category of data collected). Row 8 is re-derived on that, not on
a field that does not exist and not on a venue flow that does not exist.

### Correction 2 — /p is deliberately search-indexable (R-031)

Version 0.1 modelled every published surface as token-bound, non-indexed and
revocable, and stated that Signal Studio "refuses to ship a crawlable or
open-to-the-web mode at all."

**Verified wrong.** `app/src/app/p/[slug]/page.tsx` is "the public read-only render of
any published workspace." Its own comments state **"Server-rendered for indexing"**
and **"this is a public, indexable, rarely-changing page hit by crawlers and social
unfurls."** It uses ISR (`revalidate = 60`), emits OpenGraph and Twitter cards and a
search-snippet description, and requires no token.

`app/src/app/robots.ts` disallows `/app`, `/s`, `/share`, `/redeem`, `/welcome` and
`/api`. **`/p` and `/embed` are not on that list**, and `/p` sets no `noindex`.

**Consequence.** A couple publishing a workspace containing guests' and suppliers'
names is publishing it to search engines. `/share/[token]` and `/s/[token]` are
token-bound and disallowed to crawlers. `/p` and `/embed` are neither. Every
publication row is re-derived on that distinction, and the largest unresolved
divergence in v0.1 collapses for `/p`.

## 1. The role conclusion, restated per operation

Version 0.1 asserted a system-wide label: *"Signal Studio is a controller everywhere
and a processor nowhere."* That overstates what can be defended. **EDPB 07/2020
assesses controllership processing operation by processing operation**, and a blanket
systemic claim invites one counter-example to unravel the whole map.

### The conclusion as it should be stated

> **Signal Studio is a controller of every processing operation identified in this
> map, each assessed separately. It is a processor of none of them.** [INTERP, on
> [CODE] and [TEXT] foundations]

Scope note: this map covers the operations listed in §3. It does not pre-decide the
role for any operation not listed. New features get triaged, not assumed.

### The lead argument: essential means

**EDPB 07/2020 reserves the essential means to the controller**: what data is
collected, how long it is kept, who has access, which categories of data subject are
involved, and who the recipients are. On every operation in §3, Signal Studio decides
those things and no one instructs it. [INTERP]

Concretely and verifiably:

| Essential means | Who decides | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Retention periods | Signal Studio | `studio/content/hq/decisions/gdpr-data-lifecycle-policy.md` item 3 [CODE] |
| Access model and who can read what | Signal Studio | `studio/docs/privacy-permission-matrix.md` [CODE] |
| Which fields reach a sponsor | Signal Studio | sponsor allowlist, same file [CODE] |
| Which fields reach the open web | Signal Studio | `app/src/lib/public-task.ts` allowlist [CODE] |
| Whether an indexable publication mode exists at all | Signal Studio | `app/src/app/p/[slug]/page.tsx`, `app/src/app/robots.ts` [CODE] |
| A category of data the product asks people to collect | Signal Studio | `app/src/lib/templates.generated.ts:108` [CODE] |

This derivation is factually robust, survives Correction 1, and **does not depend on
the couple's status at all**. That is why it leads.

### Supporting note: the Recital 18 route, demoted

Version 0.1 led with: the couple is exempt, Recital 18 puts the provider of the means
in scope, Art 4(8) makes processor status derivative, therefore controller.

**That syllogism does not carry the weight v0.1 put on it.** Recital 18's second
sentence applies the Regulation to *"controllers or processors which provide the means
for processing personal data for such personal or household activities."* It **names
processors expressly**, in exactly this position. [TEXT] The argument reads a word out
of the sentence it depends on.

What the route still does usefully: it confirms Signal Studio is **in material scope**
even though the couple is not. It does not, on its own, establish which role. Kept as
a supporting note. [TEXT]

### Decoupling the couple's status

Version 0.1 made Signal Studio's role depend on the couple's exemption. That is the
wrong dependency direction: if the exemption falls, the foundation falls with it.

**Signal Studio's role is established first, from essential means.** The couple's
status is a separate question that affects only whether a **second** controller stands
alongside — which changes Art 26 questions and the couple's own exposure, not Signal
Studio's role.

On the couple's status, one limb v0.1 never tested is live and is recorded here as
open: Art 2(2)(c) requires the activity be purely personal or household **"with no
connection to a professional or commercial activity."** [TEXT] The couple is using a
venue-sponsored commercial service inside a paid booking relationship. Version 0.1
tested only the Ryneš narrowness limb and the publication limb. **The
commercial-connection limb is untested and is Open Question 4.** If the couple is a
controller of guest data, they need their own Art 9 condition, and the answer given to
a guest in Scenario 1 changes.

### Why "processor" is the wrong label, plainly

**"Processor" is the most attractive and least accurate label available to this
business.** A data-processing agreement naming Signal Studio as processor would be
wrong on its face rather than protective, because roles follow the factual
circumstances and not the label in an agreement. [INTERP, EDPB 07/2020]

`gdpr-data-lifecycle-policy.md` item 6 calls Clerk, Stripe, Turso and Vercel
**"sub-processors."** The prefix *sub-* presupposes a processor role above them that
this map rejects. That word choice is the fingerprint of the self-image being
corrected here. See §9.

### Standing triage rule for new features

Before any new feature ships, answer three questions in writing and record the answer:

1. **Name the processing operation.** Not the feature. The operation.
2. **Who decides its purpose, and who decides its essential means?** (what data, how
   long, who sees it, which people.)
3. **Assign the role on that answer**, per operation, not by inheriting this map.

If the answer is "Signal Studio decides," Signal Studio is the controller of it,
whatever the contract says.

this: for **staff accounts**, the two parties pursue **separate purposes over the same
data** and there is no converging decision on *why* the processing happens. The venue
staffs its business; Signal Studio operates an authenticated product. For
**invitations**, there is a **shared commercial purpose** in activation which both
parties pursue and from which both benefit, and the venue supplies the identifiers.
That is the converging decision Wirtschaftsakademie (C-210/16) and Fashion ID
(C-40/17) look for. [INTERP, CASE] If that distinction does not persuade, the fallback

own basis, outside this system. Signal Studio is not a controller of it and the venue
is not a controller of anything in row 8. **This closes v0.1's Open Question 2.**

## 4. Row 8 re-derived — Article 9 data in an unstructured store (R-017)

**This row is the reason the map exists, and version 0.1 got its mechanism wrong.**

### What is actually true

There is no dietary field. There is no allergy field. There is no accessibility field.
There is no guest schema and no venue transfer. [CODE — grep of `app/src/server/db/schema.ts`
and every module schema]

What exists is:

1. **A private workspace that is an unstructured free-text store.** Task titles, task
   descriptions and notes take arbitrary text. Nothing constrains what goes in.
2. **A shipped wedding template with a task titled "Collect final dietary notes"**,
   tagged `["catering","guests"]`. [CODE — `app/src/lib/templates.generated.ts:108`]

So a couple following the product's own instruction types *"Aoife — coeliac, Dad needs
step-free access"* into a task title, a description or a note. That is where Article 9
data enters this system, and it is the only way it enters.

### Why this still makes Signal Studio a controller of it

Version 0.1's route to controllership here ran through the marketing page and is gone.
The route that survives is narrower and stronger:

**Shipping a template task that instructs the collection of dietary notes determines a
category of data collected. Deciding what categories of data are collected is an
essential means, reserved to the controller under EDPB 07/2020.** [INTERP, on [CODE]]

Signal Studio did not merely host a store into which a user happened to put health
data. It shipped an instruction to go and get it. That is a design decision about *why
the data exists*, which is participation in determining the purpose. The conclusion
holds, on a much narrower base than v0.1 claimed, and it is re-derived here rather than
inherited.

### Is it all Article 9 data? No, and that matters

Version 0.1 treated the whole category as uniform and drove a heavier remediation than
the facts support.

- *"Vegetarian by preference"*, *"doesn't like fish"* — **not** Article 9. Ordinary
  personal data.
- *"Coeliac"*, *"nut allergy"*, *"needs a wheelchair space"* — data concerning health
  under Art 4(15). [TEXT] Lindqvist (C-101/01) is direct authority that a casual
  mention of a person's health condition is special-category data, not an edge case.
  [CASE]
- *"Halal"*, *"kosher"*, *"no pork"* — reveals religious belief indirectly. C-184/20
  (OT) supports indirect revelation. [CASE]

**The exposure is real but narrower than v0.1 stated**, and splitting the category is
itself data minimisation a regulator recognises.

### The Article 9(2) analysis, re-run

For the Article 9 subset that Signal Studio holds today, **no condition in Art 9(2) is
available.** [TEXT]

| Condition | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| **9(2)(a) explicit consent** | The only realistic candidate, and the guest is never asked. The couple cannot give it on the guest's behalf. |
| **9(2)(c) vital interests** | Covers an emergency. A planning record months ahead is not one. |
| **9(2)(e) manifestly made public** | The guest did not make it public. The couple did, if anyone. |
| **9(2)(f) legal claims**, **9(2)(h) healthcare** | Do not apply on these facts. |

Article 9 is a **prohibition lifted only by a listed condition**. A contract clause, a
privacy notice or a policy paragraph does not close this. [TEXT]

### Why version 0.1's two exits do not work

Version 0.1 proposed two exits and both were framed around a field that does not exist.

- **"Remove the field entirely"** is a **no-op**. There is no field. Deleting the
  template task title changes nothing: the couple types the same sentence into any
  note.
- **"Keep fields structured rather than free text"** describes a product that has not
  been built.
- A guest-supplied channel alone **opens a clean channel without closing the dirty
  one**.

### The fix set, re-derived on the real mechanism

Four parts, and only all four together address how the data actually arrives.

**(a) Ship a guest-supplied structured channel.** The guest supplies their own data,
so there is a right place to put it. State both bases explicitly, because Article 9
conditions are **additive to Article 6** and v0.1 never named the Art 6 leg:

- **Art 6(1)(a) + Art 9(2)(a)** for a declared allergy, medical or accessibility
  requirement. Explicit, specific, granular, withdrawable, with withdrawal wired to
  actual deletion.
- **Art 6(1)(f)** for an ordinary preference field (vegetarian, no fish) and for the
  contact fields on the same form.
- Two bases on one screen, implemented deliberately rather than discovered at build
  time.

**(b) Steer free text away from the dirty channel.** Retitle the template task from
*"Collect final dietary notes"* to something that points at the guest form rather than
instructing the couple to type it in. This is the smallest change on the list and it
removes the product's own instruction to create the problem.

**(c) Govern the residual honestly.** Free-text Article 9 data **will still exist**,
because the store is unstructured and always will be. Do not claim a condition that
does not exist. Instead:

- A hard **no-publication rule at title and tag level**, in code, with a regression
  test. Not a clause. Row 10a shows why: one Settings toggle currently puts a task
  title on an indexable page.
- **No venue egress**, ever, enforced server-side.
- **Shortest retention in the system**, ending at the wedding date plus a short stated
  window, and never entering Keepsake.
- A **documented harm-reduction position with a stated end date**, recorded as an
  accepted exposure rather than a claimed compliance.

**(d) Add this as a fourth option on the founder gate.** Version 0.1's gate offered
three options, none of which addressed how the data arrives. See §11, decision 2.

### The counter-intuitive consequence, worth naming before the build starts

**Shipping the guest-supplied channel *increases* the direct-notice obligation.** Once
Signal Studio holds a guest's email address, the "disproportionate effort" exception at
Art 14(5)(b) is no longer available for that person, and direct Art 14 notice becomes
feasible and therefore owed. [TEXT] The fix is still right. It is not free.

## 5. Row 10a and 10b re-derived — publication (R-031)

Version 0.1 modelled one publication surface. There are four, and they split into two
legally different things.

### 10b — token-bound publication: `/share/[token]` and `/s/[token]`

This is the surface version 0.1 described, and for it the v0.1 analysis largely holds.
Bounded audience, revocable, crawler-excluded in `robots.ts`, `/s` served
`force-dynamic` with `no-store`. Art 6(1)(f) on a defined circle is arguable. The
couple's exemption is arguable.

**One verified defect.** The "256-bit token stored only as a SHA-256 hash" control in
`privacy-permission-matrix.md` is true of the **Timeline audience** path and **false of
the Tasks share links a couple actually sends**, which store the token in plaintext as
the primary key and resolve by raw equality. [CODE — `app/src/server/db/schema.ts:713`]
For a person exposed on a shared page, the control being relied on is a property of a
different feature. Migrate `share_links` (and `pending_invites`, which stores plaintext
email alongside) to a hashed lookup, or restate the matrix per surface with an honest
column showing which controls hold where.

### 10a — open, indexable publication: `/p/[slug]` and `/embed/[slug]`

**This surface is not in the matrix, was not in version 0.1, and changes the answer.**

The facts, all [CODE]:

- `/p/[slug]` requires **no token**, is **server-rendered for indexing**, emits
  OpenGraph and Twitter cards and a search-snippet description, and is **not in the
  `robots.ts` disallow list** and sets **no `noindex`**.
- `/embed.js` lets **any third-party website** frame a couple's workspace with one
  script tag.
- Publication is **workspace-level**. One Settings toggle publishes **every task**.
- The published DTO includes **titles and tags**.
- The page carries **no controller name, no privacy link, no objection route, no
  removal request**.
- The page's footer exists to close an **SEO and virality loop** for Signal Studio's
  own user acquisition, and says so in its own source comment.

#### Consequence 1 — the couple's household exemption fails on `/p`, on its face

Lindqvist (C-101/01) holds that loading personal data onto an internet page reachable
by an indefinite number of people takes the processing outside the household
exemption. [CASE] A page **built to be indexed by search engines** is that, with no
argument required.

Version 0.1 recorded a large unresolved divergence over whether Signal Studio is sole
controller of a mechanism (Position A) or a joint controller of the act of publication
(Position B), and left it open on the assumption of a token-bound audience.

> **For `/p` and `/embed`, Position A is factually foreclosed.** There is no "defined
> private circle" to argue about. The divergence survives only for `/share` and `/s`
> (row 10b).

The founder decision that follows is therefore **not** "which reading do you prefer."
It is a build decision: **add `/p` and `/embed` to the robots disallow and set
`noindex`, or accept that the couple and Signal Studio are publishing guests' and
suppliers' names to search engines.** See §11, decision 3.

#### Consequence 2 — Signal Studio's own purpose in the publication is documented in the source

Position B's decisive claim in v0.1 was that Signal Studio takes its own benefit from
publication because shared artefacts drive discovery of new users. Version 0.1 treated
that as an interpretive assertion. **It is written in the repository.**
`published-footer.tsx` documents its purpose as closing the "SEO ↔ virality loop", and
it ships on every `/p` page. [CODE]

That is an **independent acquisition purpose in the act of publication**, self-documented,
which on Fashion ID (C-40/17) reasoning is what makes joint controllership the honest
label for the publication stage. [CASE, INTERP]

#### Consequence 3 — version 0.1's "do not write the clause" advice is struck

Version 0.1 recommended: *"Do NOT write an Art 26 arrangement with couples into the
terms until the characterisation is decided, because publishing that clause is itself
an admission of Position B."*

**That recommendation is withdrawn.** It advised withholding a disclosure specifically
to avoid admitting something the codebase already documents. From the position of a
guest or a depicted person it is a decision to keep them uninformed for litigation
posture, and it is the one place in v0.1 that protected Signal Studio rather than the
people the map is about.

Replace it with the honest version: **tell the couple at first publish, in plain words,
that the page is public and indexable, that Signal Studio has its own interest in it,
and that publishing changes the couple's own position.** If Position A is to remain
live for any surface, the acquisition CTA comes off the published page first. The
clause follows the product decision, not the other way round.

#### Consequence 4 — publication is workspace-level, so two v0.1 controls do not exist

- Version 0.1, Scenario 2: *"promotion of any item naming a third party is a deliberate
  step, not a one-click action."* **False today.** One toggle publishes everything.
  [CODE]
- Version 0.1, row 7: *"opinion-style notes never leave the workspace."* **Not
  enforceable today** for titles and tags. Descriptions are correctly excluded by
  `public-task.ts`, which is a real control and is credited, but a subjective
  judgement about a named professional in a task **title** publishes with the rest.
  [CODE]

Required: **per-item publish opt-in**, or an explicit statement in the map and in the
product that publishing a workspace publishes every task in it.

#### Consequence 5 — nobody affected can find anyone

This is the single cheapest and highest-value item in the whole map, and version 0.1
buried it as founder decision 9 of 15, framed as "who is the named contact" rather than
"the published page carries no route today."

**Ship a footer block on `/p`, `/embed`, `/s` and `/share` before the Founding 25:**

1. Controller name.
2. A monitored contact address.
3. One sentence of Art 14 notice, linking to a stable privacy URL.
4. A **"remove me from this page"** link that resolves **without an account**.

Add an identity rule for non-account requesters that does not require collecting more
data about them than is already held: match on the data already held, and act on
published surfaces **without proof of identity where the request is only to remove**.
Art 12(6) allows requesting further information to confirm identity; it does not
require inventing an identity check that harvests more data than the request. [TEXT]

## 6. Rows 9 and 11 — children, and photographs

### Row 9 — children's data

Guest lists routinely contain children: names, ages, meal choices, allergy notes,
flower girls and page boys.

**Article 8 and s.31 of the Data Protection Act 2018 are not the operative rule for a
child on a guest list.** [TEXT] Those provisions govern information society services
offered **directly to a child**. The child here is not the user. What governs is
Recital 38's requirement of specific protection and the ordinary Art 6(1)(f) balance,
which Art 6(1)(f) itself weights against Signal Studio by expressly naming children.

Any child allergy or accessibility note still needs an independent Article 9 condition,
which can only come from the responsible adult.

**Hard product rules:**

- No child data in any venue-facing evidence.
- No identifiable child on any published surface.
- No children's data carried into Keepsake.
- The shortest retention of any category in this map.
- Child dietary data supplied only by the responsible adult through the guest channel,
  never typed in by the couple.

**Where Art 8 and s.31 *do* apply, the shipped answer is wrong.** They apply to the
**account holder**, to whom the service is offered directly.
`app/src/app/privacy/page.tsx:165` states *"Tasks is for people thirteen and older."*
The Irish digital age of consent under s.31 DPA 2018 is **16**. [CODE + TEXT] Version
0.1 listed this as an open question in a document whose stated method was to verify
code facts. It is closed here: **raise the stated minimum to 16 for Ireland and the
EU, or state which lawful basis is relied on for 13-to-15-year-old account holders and
why s.31 is not engaged.** For a wedding product, 16 costs nothing.

### Row 11 — photographs, restated as a forward constraint

**An ordinary photograph is not biometric data.** Special-category status arises under
Art 4(14) and Recital 51 only where processing uses **specific technical means allowing
unique identification**. [TEXT] Signal Studio does not do that.

**Standing product rule: never ship face detection, auto-tagging or face grouping.**
That single feature would convert every photograph in the system into Article 9 data.

**Correction to version 0.1's severity ranking.** Version 0.1 called a published
photograph of a child "the highest-severity item in this whole system." **No photograph
or attachment renders on any published surface today.** [CODE — `app/src/components/published/`;
`wedding-theme.tsx` renders titles and tags only] Version 0.1 verified two code facts
and asserted this third category without checking it.

Row 11 is therefore a **forward constraint on a feature not yet shipped**, and the
severity ranking transfers to the vector that does exist:

> **The highest-severity shipped item in this system is a named guest's name and health
> hint sitting in a task title on a search-indexable page.** [CODE]

If photographs are later added to a published surface, this row activates and the
child-photograph rule becomes the top item again.

## 7. Row 13 — venue analytics, and the boundary sentence

### "Aggregate" is not "anonymous"

**The venue-facing adoption figure is not anonymous data.** Recital 26 measures
anonymity against means reasonably likely to be used **by the controller or by another
person**. [TEXT] The venue is that other person, and it holds the re-identification
key: it knows exactly which couples booked with it. A venue with six weddings this
quarter re-identifies a 50% adoption rate instantly. And *"three of your six couples
activated"* tells the venue something specific about the three who did not, which is
the actual privacy harm. Not naming them does not fix it.

Aggregation over a set the recipient already holds the key to is presentation, not
anonymisation. [INTERP]

**Required controls:**

- A minimum cohort floor, with cells below the threshold **suppressed rather than
  rounded**.
- **No per-couple activity signal of any kind.** No last-active, no task counts, no
  content signals.
- No filter combination that can narrow a view to a single couple.
- The suppression rule documented and covered by a **regression test**, not left to
  query discipline.

This has a commercial cost. A founding venue paying EUR 1,000 will see suppressed cells
in a report it paid for. That is a founder decision.

### The boundary sentence needs three clauses, not one

Version 0.1 called this its most useful output: *"from redemption, Signal Studio is
sole controller and the venue sees nothing beyond the sponsor allowlist."* But row 13
sends the venue post-redemption activation and adoption reporting, and §7 establishes
that reporting is not anonymous. **As drafted, the clause would be false on the face of
the product.**

Draft it as three clauses:

1. **Joint controllership ends at redemption.** From that moment the venue is not a
   joint controller of the workspace or anything in it. (Fashion ID stage-limiting.)
   [CASE]
2. **No workspace content passes to the venue at any time.** Enforced server-side. The
   sponsor allowlist in `privacy-permission-matrix.md` is exhaustive.
3. **Suppressed activation reporting continues after redemption**, under a named role
   and a named basis, subject to the §7 controls.

**And the consequence version 0.1 omitted entirely: couples must be told under Art 13,
at signup, that activation data about them goes to the venue.** [TEXT] That disclosure
does not exist today.

**Art 26(3) cuts both ways.** A couple can bring a rights request to either party.
"The venue holds the relationship" is not a defence for Signal Studio, and "Signal
Studio runs the software" is not a defence for the venue. [TEXT]

## 8. The four scenario tests

A role map that cannot answer a real question is not finished. **One answers clearly.
Two answer partly. One cannot be answered.**

### Scenario 1 — a guest asks for their dietary requirement to be erased

**ROLE CLEAR. OUTCOME PARTLY CLEAR. ROUTE NOT BUILT.**

**Who answers:** Signal Studio, directly. Not the couple, who is inside the household
exemption for private planning. Not the venue, which holds none of this data (Note c).
Where a joint role applies, Art 26(3) means Signal Studio cannot redirect the request
either. [TEXT]

**What the answer is.** Version 0.1's shape was *"route the private-content request to
the couple with a deadline."* **That is withdrawn.** It sends the guest's request back
to the person who entered their health data without asking, and it makes the couple the
decider of whether the guest's objection succeeds. A controller cannot discharge
Arts 15–20 by delegating to a third party, and cannot rely on a couple's cooperation to
meet the Art 12(3) deadline. [TEXT]

**The corrected shape:**

1. **On receipt, restrict processing under Art 18** pending resolution.
2. **Act unilaterally and immediately on any published surface**, regardless of the
   couple. On `/p` this is urgent, because the page is indexed. Removal must reach the
   ISR cache and any search-engine cached copy.
3. **Ask the couple to remove it from private content, with a deadline.**
4. **If the deadline passes, Signal Studio executes the erasure itself.**
5. **Hold a suppression record** so the same data cannot be silently re-added or
   exported onward.
6. **Confirm the outcome to the guest.**

**Step 4 overrides the "we don't read your workspace" principle for Article 9 data
specifically**, and it does so because there is no Art 9(2) condition for holding it in
the first place (§4). The map concedes that and follows it through.

**The unresolved design constraint this creates, which version 0.1 never named.**
§9 promises Signal Studio "does not read" the couple's workspace. A controller that
cannot reach the data it controls cannot answer a data subject. The two commitments
collide and one must give:

- **(a)** Reword the promise from *"we can't"* to *"we don't, except on a logged,
  narrow, request-triggered basis"*, and build that access path with an audit record
  the couple can see; **or**
- **(b)** Move third-party and special-category data out of the free-text store into a
  separately governed store Signal Studio can act on directly.

**Option (b) is the same build as the §4 guest-supplied channel.** That is an argument
for doing it once.

**What does not exist today:** no intake route, no identity check, no response
commitment, and no contact address on any surface a guest would reach (§5).

### Scenario 2 — a supplier objects to being named in a task

**ROLE CLEAR. CONTAINMENT NOT BUILT.** *(Downgraded from v0.1's "CLEAR".)*

The Art 21 objection bites Signal Studio's Art 6(1)(f) basis for hosting the data.
[TEXT] Signal Studio responds directly; the couple is not the route. Suppliers usually
want accuracy under Art 16 rather than erasure, and a supplier is the one affected
person in this map who can find the service, knows how to complain, and will.

**Why this is no longer marked clear.** Version 0.1's operative protections were
*"promotion of any item naming a third party is a deliberate step, not a one-click
action"* and *"opinion-style notes never leave the workspace."* **Neither is built.**
Publication is workspace-level and titles are published. [CODE] A subjective judgement
about a named professional, typed into a task title, goes to an indexable page with one
toggle.

From the supplier's position the answer is: the role is clear, and the containment is a
sentence in a document rather than a rule in the product. It becomes clear when the
per-item publish opt-in from §5 exists.

### Scenario 3 — a venue asks to see a couple's data

**CLEAR. This is the strongest answer in the map, and it survives both corrections.**

**Refused, with no discretion.** There is no lawful basis, it contradicts what the
couple was told, and it breaches purpose limitation at Art 5(1)(b). [TEXT] What the
venue bought is invitation administration, activation evidence and suppressed aggregate
adoption evidence, and nothing else.

The sponsor allowlist in `studio/docs/privacy-permission-matrix.md` is exhaustive:
activation ID and state; invitation state and timestamps; entitlement tier and
validity; sponsoring organisation ID; sponsor-local reference; consent receipt version
and timestamp. Workspace label, primary date and ceremony information require a
separate field-level consent grant. Couple names, private dates, Notes, Tasks,
unpublished Timeline content, collaborators and attachments are excluded by default.
Consent is versioned and revocable. [CODE]

**Enforced server-side. UI visibility is never an authorisation control.** [CODE — the
matrix says so in its own words.]

**One qualification added by this pass.** If the couple has published to `/p`, the
venue can read the workspace's task titles the same way anyone can, by finding the
page. The refusal is a refusal to *disclose*. It is not a technical guarantee of
confidentiality once the couple has published. That is a further reason `/p`'s
indexability is a decision and not a detail.

### Scenario 4 — a couple separates and both want the workspace

**CANNOT ANSWER. The map has no decided rule, and this pass does not create one.**

Each party is a data subject of their own contributions, and both hold Art 15 and
Art 20 rights over what they put in, so one party's Art 17 request over shared content
collides directly with the other's rights. [TEXT] Signal Studio should not adjudicate
ownership of a relationship.

Only one of the two v0.1 derivations addressed this at all. Its proposal — freeze every
public surface, give both parties an export, refuse destructive action on either
party's unilateral instruction, and hold that position under a written rule rather than
improvising during a dispute — is a sound starting point, and it is **one lane's
proposal, not a founder decision, and it has not been adversarially tested**.

**Correction 2 makes it harder, not easier.** "Freeze every public surface" reads
differently when the public surface is an **indexed page**. Freezing an indexable page
leaves it indexed. The rule needs a third state: **unpublish and de-index on notice of
a dispute**, which is itself a destructive action taken on one party's instruction, and
therefore contradicts the rest of the proposed rule.

The map states plainly: **it cannot answer this scenario.** It is a founder decision
that must be made before it goes into the couple terms, not during a dispute.

### Verdict

**The map is not finished.** It now answers who is responsible in every scenario. It
answers *what happens* in one of four cleanly, two partly, and one not at all. In
every incomplete case the gap is a **missing build and a missing founder decision**,
not a missing legal analysis.

## 9. Responsibilities in plain English

### Signal Studio is responsible for

- **Everything it decided.** Where the data lives, how it is secured, who can reach it,
  how long it is kept, **what the product asks people to collect**, whether an
  indexable publication mode exists, and what happens at the end of the term.
- **The people in a couple's workspace who never signed up.** Guests, family members
  and suppliers named by a couple are owed a route to ask what is held and to have it
  removed or corrected, and that route runs to Signal Studio directly, not through the
  couple and not through the venue.
- **Being reachable.** A named contact for data-protection requests, reachable by
  someone who has never used the product and has never heard of Signal Studio. **This
  does not exist today on any surface.**
- **Answering fast on anything published.** A person shown on a public page must be
  able to get removed without having to find the couple, and removal must be complete,
  including the ISR cache, social unfurl cards and search-engine caches.
- **Keeping the venue out.** The venue never reads private planning content. Enforced
  server-side.
- **Its own vendors**, individually. See below.
- **Telling the truth about what the product does**, including on the marketing pages
  and including that `/p` is indexed.

### Signal Studio is not responsible for

- **What a couple chooses to write about their own family and friends** in private
  planning, beyond hosting it, securing it and being able to act on a third party's
  request about it.
- **Deciding who is right when two owners fall out.** (See Scenario 4. There is no rule
  yet.)
- **What a venue does with data it collects itself.** Once a venue collects dietary
  information directly from a guest under its own catering relationship, that is the
  venue's processing, on the venue's own basis.
- **How far a couple forwards a token link** for `/share` and `/s`. Signal Studio
  controls the mechanism.

**Removed from version 0.1:** the claim that Signal Studio *"refuses to ship a
crawlable or open-to-the-web mode at all."* That sentence is false. `/p` is a
crawlable, open-to-the-web mode, shipped deliberately.

### Vendor roles, recorded individually

Version 0.1's blanket correction ("where Signal Studio is controller, they are
processors") replaced one wrong label with another.

| Vendor | Role |
|---|---|
| Turso (database) | Processor. Art 28(3) contract required. |
| Vercel (hosting) | Processor. Art 28(3) contract required. |
| Stripe (billing) | **Both.** Processor for payment processing on instruction; **independent controller** for fraud, AML and its own regulatory obligations, per its own terms. Art 28(3) for the processor leg, **Art 13 disclosure for the controller leg**. |
| Clerk (identity) | Processor for authentication; check its terms for any independent-controller leg. |

`gdpr-data-lifecycle-policy.md` item 6 calls all four **"sub-processors."** That word
presupposes a processor role above them that this map rejects.

### Signal Studio does not claim

- That it is GDPR compliant.
- That any part of this project has been reviewed or approved by a solicitor, an
  accountant, or a regulator.
- That any retention period in this product is permanent, unlimited or open-ended.
- That Art 14(5)(b) "disproportionate effort" disposes of the guest-transparency duty.

Crypto-shredding a hashed identifier does not erase free-text content. A guest's name

information" Art 14(5)(b) requires before the exception is available at all.** [TEXT]

### 10.5 No breach procedure exists (row 16)

A breach touching guest health data triggers **Art 34 communication to data subjects
Signal Studio cannot contact**. That is the identical capability gap already identified
for Art 14, with a **72-hour clock** attached. [TEXT] It is one of the first things a
supervisory authority asks for.

Write a one-page procedure: who declares a breach, who owns the 72-hour clock, the
stable public-notice URL doubling as the Art 34(3)(c) public communication route, and
the sole-founder cover question.

### 10.6 Art 30(5) and Art 37, considered

- **Art 30(5) does not exempt this operation** from keeping records of processing.

- **Art 37 — no DPO is required.** Not a public authority; core activities are not

  not "on a large scale" at 25 venues. [TEXT] Recorded because *"considered, and here

### 10.7 The DPIA has one date

Version 0.1 stated it two ways ("before the founding-25 launch" and "before the first
real couple redeems"). Art 35(1) requires it **prior to the processing**. [TEXT]

> **Fix the date: before any real guest data enters the system.** If pilot data is
> already in, it is already late.

The trigger is not scale. It is special-category data, children's data and publication
of identifiable content concerning people who never contacted the service. Check it
against the **Irish DPC's published Art 35(4) list**, which is the citable authority for
an Irish controller and independently catches special-category data concerning
vulnerable persons.

## 11. Founder decisions

Reordered from version 0.1 by **harm reduced per hour of work**. Version 0.1 put the
cheapest and highest-value item ninth.

**1. Ship a data-protection footer on every published surface. [Do this first.]**
Controller name, monitored contact address, one sentence of Art 14 notice linking to a
stable privacy URL, and a "remove me from this page" link that resolves without an
account, on `/p`, `/embed`, `/s` and `/share`. Today none of these exists on any
surface. This is hours of work, it closes the most direct harm in the system, and it is
the cheapest item on the list. **Needs a date, before the Founding 25.**

**2. The R-017 exit — the launch gate, with a fourth option added.** The private
workspace is an unstructured store that will contain Article 9 data about third parties
by design and unavoidably, and Signal Studio ships a template instructing its
collection. Choose:
 - **(a)** Guest-supplied structured channel under Art 6(1)(a) + Art 9(2)(a), with a
   separate ordinary-preference field under Art 6(1)(f).
 - **(b)** Retitle the template task so the product stops instructing free-text
   collection.
 - **(c)** Ship as-is with the exposure recorded and accepted.
 - **(d) [new, and the only option that matches the mechanism]** All of (a) and (b),
   plus govern the irreducible residual free text by retention, a hard no-publication
   rule in code at title and tag level, no venue egress, and a documented
   harm-reduction position with a stated end date.
 **Version 0.1's "remove the field" option is withdrawn. There is no field.**

**3. Is `/p` indexable, or is it not? [R-031]** Add `/p` and `/embed` to the
`robots.ts` disallow and set `noindex`, or accept that a couple publishing a workspace
publishes guests' and suppliers' names to search engines. This is a build decision, not
a preference. It decides whether the couple's household exemption survives publication,
whether Position A of the largest v0.1 divergence is available at all, and what has to
be said to couples at first publish.

**4. Per-item publish opt-in, or an honest statement.** One Settings toggle currently
publishes every task in the workspace. Either build the per-item opt-in, or state
plainly in the product and in this map that publishing a workspace publishes everything
in it. Two v0.1 controls (Scenario 2's "deliberate step", row 7's "opinion notes never
leave") do not exist without this.

**5. Controller identity on day one.** Today the controller is the founder as a natural
person, not a company. Art 13 requires the controller's true identity, so the notice
cannot name a company that does not yet exist. Either name the founder now and update
on incorporation, or incorporate before the first couple redeems. This has a
personal-exposure dimension (a natural person's name in a public notice) that is the
founder's call, not an analytical one. **Do not defer the disclosure.**

**6. The "we don't read your workspace" promise versus controller duties.** Reword to
*"we don't, except on a logged, narrow, request-triggered basis"* and build the audited
access path, or move third-party and Article 9 data into a separately governed store.
The second option is the same build as decision 2(a).

**7. Raise the stated minimum age to 16.** The shipped notice says thirteen; s.31 DPA
2018 says sixteen for Ireland. For a wedding product this costs nothing.

**8. The stated Keepsake period and its dormancy rule**, and which of the four
conflicting retention statements survives (§10.2). An open-ended read-only archive
containing guests' health data is the least defensible state in the system. The
Founding 25 contract wording has to carry the answer. Standing constraint: the words
"forever" and "for life" are never used, by Signal Studio or by a venue describing the
product.

**9. The minimum cohort suppression threshold for venue adoption reporting, and
acceptance of its commercial cost.** A venue paying EUR 1,000 will see suppressed cells
in a report it paid for.

**10. Will venues sign an Art 26 arrangement covering invitation and activation, and
accept in writing that their joint controllership ends at redemption?** Never tested
with a venue. It is a sales conversation before it is a drafting exercise. Draft the
boundary as the three clauses in §7, not the single sentence in v0.1.

**11. Is the DPIA a launch gate, dated before any real guest data enters the system?**

**12. The named data-protection contact, the response commitment, and who answers when
the sole founder is asleep or on a film shoot.** This is decision 1's contact address
with a person behind it.

**13. The couple-separation rule.** The map cannot answer Scenario 4. Freeze, dual
export, refuse unilateral destructive action is one lane's untested proposal and it
does not survive `/p` being indexed. Decide before it goes into the couple terms.

**14. May a venue reshare a couple's published page in its own marketing?** If yes, the
venue is an independent controller for that republication, needs its own basis, and the
couple has to have agreed. Intersects with R-020.

**15. Supersede `gdpr-data-lifecycle-policy.md` in full**, and confirm whether the
vendor agreements are executed and dated, with vendor roles recorded individually per
§9 and any non-EEA transfer checked for an Art 46 mechanism.

**16. A standing no on face detection, auto-tagging and face grouping.** The one feature
that would convert every photograph in the system into Article 9 biometric data.
Recording it as a permanent constraint now costs nothing and prevents a future build
decision being taken on aesthetic grounds.

**17. Whether to say plainly to couples that Signal Studio, not the couple, answers
guest requests and carries the controller duties.** On this map that is true. It is a
genuine differentiator against a spreadsheet. It is also a commitment that has to be
staffed by one person.

## 13. The five critical review findings, closed

Added 2026-08-04. Version 0.2. Each of the five findings that held this document in
internal review is closed below, with the code re-checked rather than the earlier
account trusted. **Two were closed by founder decision (D-033). Three were factual
errors in this document and are corrected here.** Nothing in §12 changes: those are
genuine legal divergences, not errors, and they stay open.

### Finding 2 — GA4 on public surfaces with no consent gate · CLOSED by D-033

Decided as **R-032 Option A**: no third-party analytics on any couple-facing public
surface. `/p`, `/s`, `/share` and `/embed` are excluded unconditionally. Implemented
across two passes: GA4 via a component seam plus an enforced CSP, and **Sentry**,
which was found still initialising on every route except `/s` — removing GA4 alone did
not satisfy the ratified sentence, because Sentry is a third party. Both now share one
exclusion predicate. **The viewer row's lawful basis no longer rests on a consent gate
that does not exist**, which is what made this critical.

### Finding 5 — the `/embed` over-generalisation · CLOSED by D-033 and Correction 2

`/p` and `/embed` are open surfaces; `/s` and `/share` are token-bound. R-031 was
decided as **Option B**: `/p` remains the couple's artifact surface, but a **wedding**
workspace is `noindex` by default, and search visibility becomes an explicit couple
opt-in. Row 10a's exposure is therefore materially reduced from what §5 assessed: the
Lindqvist reasoning still applies to the *act* of publishing, but the indexing that
made the exemption fail most starkly is off by default. **The opt-in has no storage
yet**, so today the default is the entire behaviour. Row 10a stays UNRESOLVED on
lawful basis; it is no longer *materially exposed* in the same way.

### Finding 1 — venue-facing reporting was modelled as future and already ships · CORRECTED

**The error.** Row 13 and §7 were written in the future tense. Venue-facing reporting
was already shipped at the time of writing: `studio/src/lib/account/live/
project-venue-usage.ts` projects it and the Account surfaces render it.

**The correction.** Row 13 describes a **live** processing operation, not a planned
one. Two consequences follow and neither was stated:
1. Its Art 30 record is required **now**, not at launch.
2. The suppression controls it depends on were **defective when this document was
   written**. R-027's floor guarded the population and not the count, so
   `presentBehavioural(1, 40)` published a statement about one identifiable couple;
   R-028's rate floor had no production caller at all. Both are now fixed and
   mutation-tested. **A live report whose suppression is defective is a disclosure,
   not a projection**, so §7's "not anonymous" conclusion was right for a stronger
   reason than it gave.
3. Still open, recorded rather than closed: `app/src/app/api/internal/partner-stats/
   route.ts` applies **no floor at all**. It is not venue-facing today — its only
   consumer is a founder-only HQ surface that sums across every sponsor — and it
   becomes a disclosure the moment per-venue adoption evidence is turned on.

### Finding 3 — the Article 9 control cannot be built as specified · CORRECTED

**The error.** The document specified a control over dietary and allergy data that
assumed a structured field to attach it to. **There is none.** No dietary field, no
venue-facing dietary flow, no route. The specified control had nothing to bind to.

**The correction, and it relocates the whole problem.** The Article 9 exposure is not
a *field*, it is an **instruction**. `app/src/lib/templates.generated.ts:108` ships a
default task reading "Collect final dietary notes", generated from
`studio/src/lib/templates/wedding-planning-workspace/tasks.ts:69`. Shipping a template
that instructs a couple to collect special-category data about guests who consented to
nothing is **a determination of an essential means**, and that is the controller act
— not the storage of it.

The control that can actually be built is therefore **at the template layer, not the
field layer**: remove or reword the instruction. That is one string in one generated
file with one canonical source, and cheaper than this document implied. The contract
test previously believed to block removal asserts a *different* sentence, in demo
fixtures.

**Not done here, deliberately.** Whether the instruction is removed, reworded, or kept
with guidance is a product decision (R-017) that E05.03 was explicitly told not to
pre-empt. What is closed is the *analysis*: the control is specifiable and buildable,
which is what this finding said it was not.

### Finding 4 — photographs in private workspaces written out of the present tense · CORRECTED

**The error.** The document treated photographs as a future capability. Attachments
exist in the schema today (`app/src/server/db/schema.ts`), and a couple can put a
photograph of anything into a private workspace right now.

**The correction.** Photographs in **private** workspaces are live personal data,
frequently about third parties, and occasionally special-category by content. They are
covered by the row 5 position, and three things follow:
1. They are **excluded from every published projection**, and this is enforced rather
   than intended: `ITEM_KEYS` is `publicId, title, date, state`, `attachments` is on
   the `FORBIDDEN_KEYS` denylist, and the validator rebuilds the DTO field by field.
2. A **standing no** on face detection, auto-tagging and face grouping is ratified in
   D-033. Both derivations reached it independently and it is no longer merely advisory.
3. Attachment storage moved to an **EU region** on 2026-08-04. It had been provisioned
   in `iad1`, United States, with the token already live — empty, so nothing was lost.

**Consequence for §11's DPIA trigger:** it fires earlier than written. The document
placed it "before any real guest data enters the product"; a couple uploading a
photograph is real third-party personal data, and that path is open today.
